What we found

01
Merchandising

Your hero scents keep selling out, and the site is leaning hard on the restock sign-up.

Blonde Maracujá, White Oud, Raspberry Oud and Amber Oud are out of stock across sizes, and some still sit front-and-centre on the homepage. Your back-in-stock flow itself is good, a clean "notify when restocked" with email capture. The opportunity isn't the mechanic; it's how often customers have to reach for it, and how much more each sign-up could be doing.

🔒
What we'd do about it
Reduce how often shoppers hit "sold out" with inventory-aware merchandising, then make each restock sign-up work harder, an expected-back date, a same-family scent that's in stock now, and first access when it lands. Projected recovery of xx% of hero-product demand.
02
Social proof

Your reviews are a goldmine, but they're not answering the questions a fragrance buyer actually asks.

You have hundreds of genuine, five-star reviews with a strong average, a real asset most brands would envy. What's missing is fragrance-specific structure: someone deciding on a scent they can't smell wants to know how long it lasts, how far it projects, and what it's right for. Those answers are in there, just scattered through the text instead of surfaced.

🔒
What we'd do about it
Let shoppers filter and sort reviews by xxxxxxxx, xxxxxxxx and occasion, surface customer photos, and lead with the proof that converts, turning a long list of praise into targeted reassurance at the exact moment of doubt.
03
Discovery

A brand called Mood, that you can't actually shop by mood.

Your navigation splits only by format: oils, sprays, diffusers. But nobody wakes up wanting "a 10ml", they want something warm, something fresh, an oud, a gift. The brand's own central idea is the most natural, and most under-used, way to help someone find their scent. Here's one way it could look.

🔒

Why it works
  • Mood leads, format follows. A shopper who wants "something warm" gets there in one move; the swatch system gives the range an instant, ownable hierarchy.
  • Note tags catch real intent. People arrive wanting "an oud" or "a vanilla", browsable notes turn the catalogue into something searchable and cross-sellable.
  • Discovery is one tap in. The quiz and discovery sets sit in the menu itself, de-risking the first purchase from the very first click.
  • Mobile keeps the hierarchy. The same mood-first logic collapses into a clean accordion, no squashed columns, thumb-friendly targets.
